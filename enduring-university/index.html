Enduring University:
Unfinished, Yet Alive

A Journey of Purpose, Renewal, and Courage

Shih Choon Fong

Read the Full Essay Download PDF
Scroll to explore
About This Essay

What does it take for a university not merely to survive but to remain faithful to its abiding purpose—across generations of change, upheaval, and renewal?

That question has followed six decades of work as scholar, leader, and advisor—across universities and industry, several continents and many institutions—and has sharpened rather than settled across all of them. Not what universities do, but what they are for. And what, in each generation, it takes to keep that purpose alive.

One truth has held across institutions that have outlasted empires: a university endures not because it possesses the truth, but because it renews the human capacities that sustain the search for it.

This essay draws on both Eastern and Western traditions—carrying from the West a sense of agency and accountability, and from the East a longer civilizational patience: rooted in the quiet knowing that what is planted with care will find its season.

It is offered at a moment when geopolitical fragmentation is redrawing borders, artificial intelligence is reshaping what it means to think and learn, and the university’s capacity to hold civilization’s deepest questions open is under pressure from every direction at once.

The Governing Framework

Curiosity, Creativity, and Courage

Three capacities give the university its inner life. Easier to name than to cultivate, and harder still to keep alive across the long arc of an institution. Select each to explore further.

Curiosity

Oxygen for the Mind

Curiosity is oxygen for the mind—fueling inquiry, sparking exploration, and deepening understanding. It compels us to ask questions, challenge assumptions, and seek insight beyond the visible horizon—perceiving possibility where others see limits, and staying alert to what remains unthought.

Yet it is also fragile: what comes naturally in childhood is not always what schooling protects—over-structured systems too easily trade open-ended inquiry for standardized outcomes, dulling the very curiosity such systems need to thrive.

Richard Feynman called it “the pleasure of finding things out”; Albert Einstein declared that it “has its own reason for existing.” It is the beginning of genuine understanding, driving us to pursue not just what is, but what could be.

Creativity

Alchemy of Imagination

Creativity is the mind’s alchemy—the essential antidote to inert ideas. In The Aims of Education, Alfred North Whitehead warns that education built on ideas passively received—never tested, applied, or thrown into fresh combinations—is not merely useless but harmful.

Nourished by curiosity, creativity grows through serendipity and experimentation—redrawing boundaries and releasing new possibilities. Across eras and traditions, figures as varied as Leonardo da Vinci bridging art and science, Zhuangzi dissolving the divide between knowing and being, Stravinsky remaking the grammar of music, and Steve Jobs marrying design and technology have each reimagined the world’s limits rather than working within them.

Yet creativity alone may remain suspended in imagination—brilliant yet unrealized. What distinguishes the vision that enters the world from the one that does not is courage.

Courage

Crucible of the Will

Courage is the crucible of the will—where conviction meets resistance, and what endures through that reckoning becomes real. Of the three capacities, courage is the least celebrated in academic life—and the one this essay dwells on longest. The reason is telling: it is the only one that costs something.

Curiosity and creativity are praised, resourced, and institutionally rewarded; courage, by contrast, is frequently invoked and rarely practiced—and that cost is seldom visible. It lives in what was not compromised, what was not conceded, what held firm when the easier path was available.

In the scholar who tells an inconvenient truth, the leader who declines a misaligned opportunity, and the institution that holds to its mission when the world offers it a different and more profitable one. Without courage, curiosity and creativity, however bright, remain unrealized.

Conscience: Courage’s Quiet Compass

Not a fourth capacity, but the inner compass the other three depend on

Across traditions—Confucius, Socrates, Rumi—the deepest counsel on courage is less about summoning it than about guiding it. What Aristotle named practical wisdom—phronesis—is the cultivated capacity to judge well in particular circumstances, guided not by rule but by a formed sense of what genuinely matters. Conscience keeps courage true to its purpose.

Central Metaphor

The Enduring University—Rooted to Renew, Courageous through Change

The tree is the essay’s unifying image: a living university anchored in purpose, sustained by culture and stewardship, and renewed through the capacities of curiosity, creativity, and courage.

“What endures is not built but grown—and unlike a machine, a tree cannot be assembled from parts, nor rushed into being. It grows from within, or not at all.”

An enduring university is a living system, from roots through trunk to canopy: steadfast in purpose, sustained by stewardship, and renewed by the capacities of curiosity, creativity, and courage (the 3Cs). Deep roots—mission, values, discernment, and conscience (the ethical compass)—ground governance and anchor steady, resilient growth; the 3Cs shape the trunk that turns purpose into practice. Blossoms, fruit, and seed signify knowledge creation, learner formation, and contributions to the public good.

Near the roots lies AI’s Yin–Yang, a foundational, co-arising pair of complementary forces—signalling that governance sits at the roots. Yin—the dark wave—signifies turbulence and risk; Yang—the green sprout—signals opportunity and renewal. This motif reminds us that AI’s perils and promises arise from the same ground, each holding the other’s seed.

Around the tree, crosscurrents swirl—rankings, market pressures, geopolitics, and bureaucracy. The visual treats them as weather: conditions to read and navigate, not determinants of the university’s course. The tree does not reshape itself around each storm. It holds, and it waits, and it grows.

A Season of Becoming

Renewal at NUS

The initiatives Professor Shih describes are best understood not as program launches but as part of a season of becoming—a period in which curiosity, creativity, and courage moved from aspiration into institutional life.

Immersive internships within global startup ecosystems—from Silicon Valley to Stockholm to Shanghai—opened new horizons and gave students the footing to act in settings where little is certain. NUS Enterprise, in parallel, nurtured startups and helped catalyze Singapore’s entrepreneurial ecosystem.

Students returned not only with startup experience or global exposure, but with sharpened instincts for inquiry, a tolerance for ambiguity, and the confidence to act before the path was fully clear. That, in essence, is what curiosity, creativity, and courage look like when they are earned rather than taught.

Over 3,600 alumni have since gone through the program across 15+ global hubs, founding more than 1,000 startups—nine of which achieved unicorn status. What began in 2002 with 14 students in Silicon Valley became a defining feature of NUS and a model for university-led entrepreneurship education worldwide.

While NOC placed students inside the world’s startup ecosystems, the University Scholars Programme (USP) cultivated a different kind of depth—through ideas, dialogue, and the sustained intellectual community of a rigorous residential program at NUS.

Its aim was complementary: interdisciplinary thinking and intellectual breadth, nurtured through student-centered learning and the daily rhythms of a community living and working closely together. Two programs, one purpose. Both cultivated habits of mind through sustained engagement with contrasting perspectives—building the agility, resourcefulness, and civic orientation that a complex world will ask of the next generation.

The NUS Tenure Reform reached deeper than program or structure—reshaping academic culture itself in ways that called for steady courage and resolve. It replaced a seniority-based model with one founded on merit and transparency: applicant-initiated, with parallel academic and administrative evaluations—a design rare in Asia at the time, and a direct challenge to the region’s prevailing rank-centric culture (官本位, the elevation of bureaucratic rank above merit).

What made it possible was trust—built through sustained dialogue, tested against long-held norms, and held through resistance. By aligning tenure with international standards, NUS strengthened internationally engaged scholarship and cross-boundary collaboration.

“No walls to talent, no walls to ideas, no walls around minds.” Beneath these initiatives lay a single unifying aim: to reshape NUS’s culture toward open inquiry and collegial trust—alive to possibilities that transcend boundaries.

That call found concrete expression in cross-faculty initiatives, joint appointments, and global partnerships. In time, more than 70% of undergraduates crossed borders—from year-long immersions to shorter stints abroad. For a city-state with a citizen population of barely four million, this was not incidental but essential: what geography could not provide, the university would seek.

The no-walls ethos faces its most demanding test in an era of geopolitical fragmentation. Singapore’s answer has been neither accommodation nor withdrawal, but principled engagement—keeping relationships broad, doors open, and options alive. For NUS, holding to that conviction, under pressure, is itself an act of institutional courage.

About the Author

Professor Shih Choon Fong is one of the most consequential university leaders Asia has produced. With a PhD in Applied Mathematics from Harvard, he began his career as a world-leading researcher in nonlinear fracture mechanics—among the most highly cited engineering researchers globally—before turning to the challenge of institutional transformation that would define his later decades.

As President of the National University of Singapore from 2000 to 2008, he led a sweeping transformation that elevated NUS into one of Asia’s premier research universities and a globally recognized institution. His vision of NUS as a “boundaryless knowledge enterprise” was realized through bold initiatives: the creation of NUS Overseas Colleges, a landmark tenure reform, and the “no-walls” philosophy that opened the university to talent, ideas, and partnerships across every boundary.

In 2008, he was appointed Founding President of the King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST) in Saudi Arabia, where he built a new research university from inception—recruiting world-class faculty and establishing the institution’s trajectory toward research excellence.

His advisory work has spanned corporations, research institutes, and governments worldwide, including service to three Presidents of the University of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. His journey across six decades, several continents, and both Eastern and Western intellectual traditions informs every page of this essay.

Enduring University: Unfinished, Yet Alive

A Journey of Purpose, Renewal, and Courage

Shih Choon Fong, University Professor, National University of Singapore

Preface Reflections Across Time and Seasons Part I Purpose: Wellspring of Enduring Excellence Part II Stewardship: Leadership, Trust, and Courage Part III Culture: Wellspring of Enduring Character and Meaning Epilogue A Curious Mind, a Steady Heart, and a Light Step

Preface: Reflections Across Time and Seasons

This essay is the work of a long journey—across universities and industry, six decades and several continents—in which the question of what a university is truly for has never quite let go. That span includes a decade in the private sector as a research leader in R&D and startups, where the pressures of innovation and enterprise sharpened instincts that no campus alone could have forged, before the longer return to academic life: from the National University of Singapore to King Abdullah University of Science and Technology, through advisory work spanning corporations and research institutes worldwide, and in service to three Presidents of the University of the Chinese Academy of Sciences.

From those years, I have drawn on both Eastern and Western traditions—carrying from the West a sense of agency and accountability, and from the East a longer civilizational view: patient in cultivation, and rooted in the quiet knowing that what is planted with care will find its season. That journey is unfinished—as any honest account of the enduring university must be. It is offered in that spirit: exploratory, alive, and open to what the next season may bring.

Six decades across universities and industry have made one truth inescapable: how deeply institutional character is shaped by mission. While corporations pursue market value, universities bear a different kind of trust—one rooted in learning, service, and stewardship. That trust is now being tested in an era of turbulence, as technological disruption and shifting political currents press upon institutions whose purposes were formed in quieter times. What a university needs to navigate such a moment is not strategy alone, but renewal—rooted in purpose, guided by hard-won judgment, and sustained by the courage to act amid uncertainty. Renewal, rightly understood, is not reinvention but evolution with continuity—holding fast to what is essential and remaining answerable to what the moment requires.

Yet the deeper test of renewal is not external. A university’s capacity for renewal depends on something closer to home: the collegial trust that binds mission to daily practice from within. In this era, that trust ranks among the most quietly eroded of institutional assets—felt most sharply in its absence. The remedy, though, is nearer than it may appear. The university’s people—many drawn to academic life not for security or status, but for the chance to pursue what genuinely matters to them—are, in themselves, the resource that institutional renewal most needs. When their aspirations align with mission, something more than efficiency is achieved: their work acquires deeper meaning, and the institution draws its deepest strength from the devotion of those who believe in what the university is for.

That commitment—and what it demands of leadership—crystallized in a university address: “A Good University Teaches; A Great University Transforms.” The conviction endures. But the decades of practice have tempered that conviction with a harder recognition: that without inner renewal, transformation risks becoming performance rather than permanence. This inward resolve—quietly rooted, steadily renewed—is what gives transformation its lasting force.

That resolve draws on three capacities—curiosity, creativity, and courage—easier to name than to cultivate, and harder still to keep alive across the long arc of an institution. Orienting all three is conscience: not a fourth capacity, but the inner compass that keeps the other three true to their purpose. They are not givens. Each must be tended—against the pressures of conformity, the fatigue of routine, and the slow drift toward the merely expedient.

It is the journey of keeping those capacities alive—in each generation, across every season of change—that this essay traces: not as a finished achievement to be defended, but as a continuing endeavor to be lived, carried forward by those willing to tend what they inherited—and to begin what they may not complete.

Part I

Purpose: Wellspring of Enduring Excellence

The enduring university is not a finished institution, but a continuing human journey—sustained across generations by purpose, renewal, and courage. It endures not because it possesses the truth, but because it renews the human capacities that keep the search for truth alive.

Its endurance depends on a culture shaped by inquiry, scholarship, and service—one in which purpose and values are woven into daily life, excellence is measured by public trust, and horizons remain open to wonder and humility. That openness—deepened by curiosity, creativity, and courage—is the ground of enduring excellence.

What, then, makes a university truly excellent—worthy, in time, of being called “great”? The question is easier asked than answered. Rankings and research output matter, but they are instruments, not the measure of excellence. Greatness, in this deeper sense, lies in excellence in service of purpose: creating knowledge, developing human potential, and serving society in ways that earn and renew public trust over time. Such excellence is aligned with purpose, guided by values, and answerable to those it serves—a standard no ranking was designed to capture, and none ever will.

Purpose so understood—the university’s reason for being—sets the horizon of lasting significance. It orients mission, sustains values, and gives direction to the university’s work across time. Largely unseen yet quietly foundational, it is the source from which inquiry, learning, and the cultivation of human potential draw their nourishment and direction—and from which the capacities that carry the university forward take root and grow.

Purpose finds expression in three movements. Inwardly, mission gives purpose direction, while values, enduring yet responsive, provide ethical grounding; together, they carry purpose into judgment, decision, and daily practice. Upward, vision clarifies the horizon—not replacing purpose but renewing and enlarging it, aligning the university’s work with what it seeks to become. Outwardly, calling carries purpose into the civic realm: serving society, widening opportunity, and stewarding the public good.

Enduring excellence, then, is not declared but practiced—expressed through inquiry, learning, and service, and tested against the commitments that give it meaning. What is needed now is not the defense of the familiar, but the renewal of excellence: an excellence that meets what this era asks of the university without surrendering what must endure.

The Ethos of Renewal: Cultivated from Within

Renewal is the university’s continuing response to changing times—not a reinvention of what it is, but a return to what it has always been called to do: faithful to purpose, attentive to what shifts, and anchored in the values that endure. What drives renewal is less strategy or structure than ethos—the inner conviction that shapes how a university thinks, works, and acts.

The 3Cs—curiosity, creativity, and courage—are the capacities from which that ethos grows: not directives imposed from above, but dispositions nurtured from within. Culture is the ground in which the 3Cs take root and spread—expressed in the ways people teach, collaborate, decide, and respond to change.

Curiosity: Oxygen for the Mind

Curiosity is oxygen for the mind—fueling inquiry, sparking exploration, and deepening understanding. It compels us to ask questions, challenge assumptions, and seek insight beyond the visible horizon—perceiving possibility where others see limits, and staying alert to what remains unthought. Yet it is also fragile: what comes naturally in childhood is not always what schooling protects.

Curiosity, once kindled, proves remarkably resilient. Richard Feynman called it “the pleasure of finding things out”; Albert Einstein declared that it “has its own reason for existing.” It is the beginning of genuine understanding, driving us to pursue not just what is, but what could be.

Creativity: Alchemy of Imagination

Creativity is the mind’s alchemy—the essential antidote to inert ideas. In The Aims of Education, Alfred North Whitehead warns that education built on ideas passively received—never tested, applied, or thrown into fresh combinations—is not merely useless but harmful. It is creativity that supplies the remedy.

Across eras and traditions, figures as varied as Leonardo da Vinci bridging art and science, Zhuangzi dissolving the divide between knowing and being, Stravinsky remaking the grammar of music, and Steve Jobs marrying design and technology have each reimagined the world’s limits rather than working within them. Yet creativity alone may remain suspended in imagination—brilliant yet unrealized. What distinguishes the vision that enters the world from the one that does not is courage.

Courage: Crucible of the Will

Courage is the crucible of the will—where conviction meets resistance, and what endures through that reckoning becomes real. Curiosity opens the question; creativity gives it form; courage determines whether it enters the world.

Of the three capacities, courage is the least celebrated in academic life—and the one this essay dwells on longest. The reason is telling: it is the only one that costs something. It lives in what was not compromised, what was not conceded, what held firm when the easier path was available: in the scholar who tells an inconvenient truth, the leader who declines a misaligned opportunity, and the institution that holds to its mission when the world offers it a different and more profitable one.

Conscience: Courage’s Quiet Compass

Yet courage, without orientation, can distort as readily as it compels—masking willfulness as conviction, and rigidity as principle. Across traditions, the deepest counsel on courage is less about summoning it than about guiding it. Confucius cautioned that courage without righteousness invites disorder. Socrates linked courage to virtue formed through unflinching self-examination. Rumi urged us to heed the inner voice attuned to truth and meaning.

What the philosophical traditions call conscience, Aristotle named practical wisdom—phronesis: the cultivated capacity to judge well in particular circumstances, guided not by rule but by a formed sense of what genuinely matters.

The Duty to Act: Courage in Academic Life

In Academic Duty (1997), Donald Kennedy—who served as Stanford’s president for over a decade—frames the professor’s obligations under headings that reveal the full institutional demand of courage: “To Serve the University,” “To Tell the Truth,” “To Reach Beyond the Walls.” These are not merely professional obligations; together, they amount to a continuing demand for courage.

Courage Lived: Curie and Lee

Marie Curie pressed forward undeterred—through barriers that were not only scientific but social, institutional, and deeply personal. Her discoveries of polonium and radium helped pave the way for life-saving advances; her refusal to patent radium embodied a courage that reached beyond the laboratory.

If Curie exemplifies courage in the pursuit of knowledge, Lee Kong Chian offers its civic-minded counterpart. He risked much of his wealth to found the Lee Foundation in pre-independence Singapore—establishing a philanthropic institution dedicated to education and societal renewal. Conviction his ground, humility his bearing, and reach his measure.

Principled Tension: Inquiry, Will, and the Between

The Socratic spirit—relentless, probing, and inexhaustible—runs deep in the university’s identity. Yet left entirely to itself, it carries a latent risk—the slide toward hesitation. Nietzsche offered the necessary counterpoint: it is not enough to ask why; one must also summon the will to decide, create, and act.

The 3Cs hold these forces in principled tension—and in doing so, give the university its deepest coherence.

The Tree: Root to Canopy

The university’s deepest architecture resolves into a single image: a tree rooted in purpose, mission, and values—its trunk shaped by curiosity, creativity, and courage, its canopy bearing the fruits of the university’s public service: knowledge created, lives shaped, and public trust earned.

And from the canopy, seeds fall—unannounced—carrying purpose forward into seasons not yet visible and renewing the journey in each generation.

Part II

Stewardship: Leadership, Trust, and Courage

If purpose gives direction and awakens conviction, stewardship turns each into habit—and habit, over time, into the lived character of an institution. One truth has held across every institution where I have worked: what endures is less what a university achieves than how deeply it lives what it believes.

Leadership, here, is less a matter of position than of attentive presence and steady engagement. Its strength lies in a clear, unifying vision: a guiding orientation—not a rigid blueprint—rooted in collegial traditions that safeguard academic freedom and shared governance.

Academic Freedom and Shared Responsibility

At its core, academic freedom is the protected capacity to teach, research, and follow ideas wherever they lead, exercised with scholarly judgment and anchored in shared responsibility. Both rest on a single, demanding proposition: that knowledge advances best when accountability is directed inward—to evidence, argument, and conscience—rather than outward to convenience or power.

The Mother Tree: Unity, Not Uniformity

Think of a mother tree—one of the ancient ones, its canopy broad, its roots deep, its reach far and wide. Beneath the surface, it sustains a network through which nourishment passes between trees, seedlings are tended, and what has been accumulated over a long life is shared across generations.

A university aspires to be what the mother tree embodies—not uniform in its parts, but unified in its purpose. Oxford has stood for nearly a thousand years, Bologna for longer still, each outlasting the empires and upheavals that sought to unmake them.

Awakening: A Season of Becoming

A defining example was the launch of NUS Overseas Colleges (NOC) and NUS Enterprise—initiatives designed to foster entrepreneurial thinking, spur exploration, and build resilience in a rapidly changing world. Students returned not only with startup experience or global exposure, but with sharpened instincts for inquiry, a tolerance for ambiguity, and the confidence to act before the path was fully clear.

Tenure Reform and the No-Walls Ethos

The NUS Tenure Reform reached deeper than program or structure—reshaping academic culture itself. It replaced a seniority-based model with one founded on merit and transparency: applicant-initiated, with parallel academic and administrative evaluations—a design rare in Asia at the time.

Beneath these initiatives lay a single unifying aim: to reshape NUS’s culture toward open inquiry and collegial trust. Its first public expression came in a 2002 welcome address: “no walls to talent, no walls to ideas, no walls around minds.”

Trust: What Made It Possible

Across these initiatives—the overseas colleges, the scholars program, the tenure reform, the no-walls ethos—one condition made each of them possible and carried each of them forward: trust. Not trust assumed at the outset, but trust earned over time through transparency, consistency, and leadership willing to be held to its own stated commitments.

Tending the Trust: Mission Against the Currents

Among the enduring currents that make stewardship demanding, two have long been familiar: the creep of bureaucracy and the pull of market logics. A third—more novel in form but no less consequential—has lately joined them: artificial intelligence.

AI carries real promise for the university: it can extend access to knowledge, free scholars from rote labor, and open new frontiers of inquiry. Yet by reshaping how knowledge is generated, transmitted, and evaluated, AI threatens to displace the very capacities the university exists to cultivate: wonder, the pleasure of discovery, and the slow formation of judgment that no algorithm can replicate.

Engagement Without Absorption

Clark Kerr observed that the campus and society were undergoing a reluctant but inexorable merger. Allan Bloom reversed the relation entirely: society is ministerial to the university. Both are right—and the tension between them is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be lived.

Called to Public Purpose

No other institution simultaneously holds academic freedom, multigenerational continuity, and the deliberate cultivation of curiosity, creativity, and courage as its primary purpose—shaping, cohort by cohort, how an entire society learns to question, imagine, and act with conscience.

Part III

Culture: Wellspring of Enduring Character and Meaning

Culture—deeper than strategy or structure—shapes how people engage with ideas, one another, and the institution itself. While strategy sets the course, culture makes it real and sustainable—turning purpose, values, and mission into lived practice where they take root, deepen, and endure.

In a 1994 Foreign Affairs conversation with Fareed Zakaria, titled “Culture Is Destiny,” Lee Kuan Yew argued that, more than politics or economics, culture ultimately shapes a community’s renewal and possibilities. What is true of societies is no less true of universities.

Charles W. Eliot captured this more than a century ago: “A university, in any worthy sense of the term, must grow from seed... It cannot be run up, like a cotton-mill, in six months, to meet a quick demand.”

Social Architecture of Renewal

From this soil, the social architecture of renewal takes shape—at once organic and intentional, grounded in the rhythms of daily life yet shaped by shared purpose and collegial trust. At NUS, it was that dimension—lived in shared laboratories, in late-evening seminars, in the daily accumulation of small collegial acts—that carried renewal deeper than any single reform could reach.

Meaning: Wonder, Humility, and Inquiry

Few have framed this quest more searchingly than Gauguin, whose great 1897 painting bears not one question but three: Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going? They are questions that do not resolve—only open further.

“To myself, I am only a child playing on the seashore, every now and then finding a smoother pebble or a prettier shell than ordinary, while vast oceans of truth lie undiscovered before me.”
—Isaac Newton

That orientation—wonder, humility, and courage held together—is what the university carries into every season of change, and what each generation inherits as the ground from which its own work begins.

Epilogue

A Curious Mind, a Steady Heart, and a Light Step

If the university is to endure, it must endure not as a finished institution, but as a continuing human endeavor—renewed across generations by purpose, courage, and the willingness to begin again.

It is this formation of conscience, carried generation by generation, that gives endurance its truest meaning. Purpose sets direction; curiosity, creativity, and courage carry it forward; culture sustains all across time. Of the many institutions that serve the public good, the university occupies a singular place: it alone makes the cultivation of curiosity, creativity, and courage—under conditions of academic freedom—its primary purpose.

Quiet Lifelong Companions

Curiosity, creativity, and courage—and conscience—are not convictions I arrived at through learning and reflection alone. They are inner truths shaped across a lifetime—not destinations reached, but quiet companions: a steady presence through every season of change.

Among these companions, curiosity has often illuminated the way—sometimes with a spirit of adventure, and at other times with a quieter attentiveness. It was curiosity in this quieter register that made me most receptive to my Buddhist mother’s reflective wisdom—her reminder not to hold too tightly to anything: things or feelings, gains or losses. She taught me that life is about traveling light—letting go of what weighs us down and lifting our gaze beyond the immediate to the horizon.

Though she has been gone for many years now, her gentle counsel still shapes how I seek clarity, let go, and live by what matters—humility, humanity, and humor—essentials light enough to travel with, steady enough to trust.

Planting for Seasons Yet to Come

What we set in motion today becomes part of a journey still unfolding—and what is truly worth doing, as Reinhold Niebuhr reminded us, need not be completed in our lifetime. Yet we begin. In that beginning, purpose is renewed. The rest we entrust.

We step forward not because the path is certain, but because our purpose is clear. We plant what we may never see in full blossom—in small acts of listening, serving, encouraging, and giving—trusting that these seeds will take root and bear fruit in seasons beyond our own.

And so the university itself lives on—not because it possesses the truth, but because it renews the human capacities that sustain the search for truth.

And so we carry forward what endures—passing it on, as it was once passed to us, in the quiet rhythms of renewal: one curious mind, one steady heart, one light step—and the courage, always, to begin again.
“What steadies us through seasons of change is not the weight we carry but the clarity we hold close: a curious mind, a steady heart, and a light step.”
Shih Choon Fong
